Learn the Content

Organized like your notes: Chapter 7, then Chapter 12, 8, and 9. Yellow boxes are hints you wrote. Red boxes are common traps.

Interest rate risk

Comes from a mismatch in asset and liability maturities. As rates change, the spread changes, and since value = PV(cash flows), equity is affected.

  • Matching maturities is problematic for FIs: it's inconsistent with their asset transformation function.
  • Refinancing risk: liabilities mature first and must be rolled over at higher rates.
  • Reinvestment risk: assets mature first and must be reinvested at lower rates.
  • Market value risk: asset and liability values move when rates move.
When interest rates change in the market, it creates volatility in net income because you can't control the interest rates.

Credit risk

Risk that promised cash flows won't be paid in full. Charge-offs were high in the 1980s, most of the 1990s, and the 2000s, and kept growing until late 2008.

  • Firm-specific credit risk: default risk tied to one borrower's own situation. It can be diversified away.
  • Systematic credit risk: default risk from economy-wide conditions, like a recession. It can't be diversified away.
  • Implications: credit screening and monitoring, diversification, loan sales, reschedulings, good bank/bad bank structures, credit derivatives.

Liquidity risk

A sudden surge in liability withdrawals may force an FI to liquidate assets in a very short time at low prices. It can generate runs, and runs can turn a liquidity problem into a solvency problem.

This contributed to the Silicon Valley Bank failure in 2023.

On the asset side, loan requests and borrowers drawing on loan commitments also create liquidity risk.

Foreign exchange risk

  • An FI may be net long or net short in various currencies.
  • Foreign and domestic returns aren't perfectly correlated, and FX rates aren't perfectly correlated across countries ($/€ can rise while $/¥ falls).
  • Undiversified foreign exposure creates FX risk.
  • Fully hedging by matching foreign assets and liabilities requires matching durations, not just maturities. Otherwise foreign interest rate risk remains.

Country or sovereign risk

A type of credit risk: foreign borrowers can't repay because their government interferes. There's often no usual recourse through the courts.

  • Example: Argentina defaulted on $130b of government debt in 2001 and was still asking for payment adjustments in 2018.
  • The FI's remaining bargaining chip is its future supply of loans, which is weak if the currency or government is collapsing.

Market risk

Risk from trading assets and liabilities when interest rates, exchange rates, and other asset prices change. Short trading horizons.

  • Present whenever an FI holds an open or unhedged long or short position and prices move the wrong way.
  • 2008-09: FIs were major mortgage buyers; rising rates in the mid-2000s drove defaults; "toxic" assets fell to deeply reduced values.
  • Implication: need for controls and for measuring risk exposure.

Off-balance-sheet risk

Risk from contingent assets and liabilities: letters of credit, loan commitments, derivatives. These directly affect future profitability.

Technology and operational risk

Loss from inadequate or failed internal processes, people, and systems, or from external events. Operational risk includes technology risk.

  • Examples: Target hack (2013), "ATM cash-out" schemes, the London Whale.
  • Innovation: ACH, CHIPS, real-time global connections (e.g., Citigroup). Benefits: economies of scale and scope.
  • Not only tech failures: employee fraud and errors count, and losses get magnified by reputation damage.

FinTech risk

Tech-enabled innovation that creates new business models or products with a material effect on financial services. The risk is that FinTech firms take customers and revenue from traditional FIs (e.g., SoFi, Transferwise, Credit Karma). It's broader than technology risk.

Insolvency risk and how risks interact

Insolvency (capital adequacy): not enough capital to absorb a sudden drop in asset value relative to liabilities. It's usually the end result of the other risks. Examples: Washington Mutual; "too big to fail" (Citigroup).

Interactions: risks are interdependent (interest rate + credit + OBS; liquidity + interest rate + credit). Discrete risks: war, terrorism, market crashes, theft, malfeasance, changes in regulatory policy.

What is liquidity risk?

The risk a bank doesn't have easy access to cash when they need it. Banks usually have a lot of illiquid assets (like credit cards or loans).

Liability side

  • Depositors cash in claims. With low cash, the FI may have to sell assets fast, which can mean a fire sale (selling illiquid assets below market price).
  • Net deposit drain = withdrawals − additions. Banks need to predict its probability distribution, including seasonality (Christmas, summer).
  • Managed by purchased and/or stored liquidity management.

Asset side

  • Loan commitments and credit lines get drawn down. Met by borrowing or using cash.
  • Commitments are "dangerously high": unused commitments to cash went from 529% (1994) to 1,015% (2008), then 609% in the crisis.
  • Interest rate and market risk can drop investment portfolio values. Tech may add liquidity; herd behavior may reduce it.

Purchased liquidity management (PLM)

Borrow to replace the lost cash: fed funds or repo markets.

  • Preserves the asset side; balance sheet size stays the same.
  • Borrowed funds cost more than deposits.
  • Deposits are insured; borrowed funds aren't necessarily.
  • In the crisis, wholesale funding dried up.
The result of PLM is lower net income because it's a more expensive way to finance.

Stored liquidity management (SLM)

Use cash reserves and sell liquid assets to meet withdrawals. DIs hold excess reserves above Fed requirements.

  • Assets shrink.
  • Requires holding excess low-rate assets.
  • Opportunity cost of excess cash and liquid assets.
  • Banks often combine both methods.
Banks lose out on the opportunity to make more on the spread since they can't make as many loans.
Key terms: purchased liquidity = borrowed funds. Stored liquidity = cash and reserves (and selling liquid securities).

Financing gap

FG = Average loans − Average (core) deposits
FG = −Liquid assets + Borrowed funds
Financing requirement = FG + Liquid assets = Borrowed funds
How much borrowing do I need to do to cover my loans?
For the project: FG = (Loans/A − Deposits/A) × A, where A = your bank's assets.

Example (Table 12-6): loans 25 − core deposits 20 = FG of 5. Check: −5 liquid + 10 borrowed = 5.

Sources and uses of liquidity

Net liquidity statement:

  • Sources: (i) cash-type assets, (ii) maximum borrowed funds available, (iii) excess cash reserves.
  • Uses: borrowed or purchased funds already used, and anything already borrowed from the Fed discount window.

Past statements help managers spot future liquidity problems.

Liquidity index

I = Σ wᵢ (Pᵢ / Pᵢ*)

P = fire-sale price, P* = fair (full) price, w = each asset's share of portfolio value. Closer to 1 = more liquid.

Example 12-1: 50% T-bills (99 vs 100) and 50% real estate loans (85 vs 92): I = 0.495 + 0.462 = 0.957. In a thin market (65 vs 92): I = 0.495 + 0.353 = 0.848.

Shortcut when P* is fair value: I = total fire-sale value ÷ total fair value. Problem set: (9 + 14 + 8 + 65) / 130 = 0.738.

New ratios (Basel III)

LCR = High-quality liquid assets / Net cash outflows over 30 days ≥ 100%
Think of it like how much can the bank cover on their own for liquidity needs.
NSFR = Available stable funding / Required stable funding > 100%
Of all of our liabilities and equity, how much is not going anywhere? Is it less than or greater than 1, and what does it mean?

Answer to your question: above 1 means the bank has more stable funding than its assets require, which is the goal. Below 1 means too much long-term lending is funded by money that could leave.

Liquidity planning

I.e., what your plan is when everything goes wrong during a crisis.
  • Make funding decisions before problems arise; find the cheapest funding mix; minimize excess reserves.
  • Delineate responsibilities: who talks to regulators, who discloses to the public.
  • List fund providers and how they'll behave: mutual and pension funds pull out faster than correspondent banks and small businesses. Allow for seasonality.
  • Size potential withdrawals over different horizons; identify private-market alternatives.
  • Set internal limits on subsidiaries' and branches' borrowing; plan the sequence of asset sales.

Bank runs

  • Triggered by concerns about solvency, failure of a related FI, or sudden shifts in investor preferences.
  • Demand deposits are first come, first served, so your place in line matters. That's what makes runs self-feeding.
  • Bank panic: a systemic, contagious run across the industry.
  • Fixes: deposit insurance (FDIC), discount window, direct actions like TARP (2008-09).
  • Not free: protection may encourage banks to take more liquidity risk.
For SVB, the government said they'd cover everything.

Level and movement of rates

  • The Fed uses open market operations to influence money supply, inflation, and rates. It mostly targets short-term rates, especially fed funds.
  • Dec 2008: target of 0 to 0.25%. First hike in 10 years: Dec 2015, then six more by June 2018.
  • Rate changes spread across countries through global markets. The Fed chair's statements can move world rates.
Grey areas on the T-bill chart are recessions. In every documented recession, rates drop.

The repricing (funding gap) model

A simple book-value cash flow model used by small US FIs. It compares interest income on assets with interest expense on liabilities over a planning period.

GAP = RSA − RSL
ΔNII = CGAP × ΔR
Gap ratio = CGAP / Total assets

Maturity buckets: 1 day; 1 day to 3 months; 3 to 6 months; 6 to 12 months; 1 to 5 years; over 5 years.

What counts as rate sensitive?

It reprices at or near current market rates within the planning period:

If something matures in the window we care about (like T-bills), or if it has a variable interest rate (like a credit card).
  • Short-term consumer loans: reprice at year-end, just make the 1-year cutoff.
  • 3-month T-bills reprice every 3 months; 6-month T-notes every 6 months.
  • 30-year floating-rate mortgages that reset every 9 months count in the 1-year gap.
  • A 30-year fixed mortgage maturing in 20 years: not rate sensitive.
Long maturity doesn't mean "not rate sensitive" if the rate resets. Check the reset date, not just the maturity.

Demand deposits: RSL or not?

Against inclusion: the explicit rate is zero by regulation, rates paid don't track market rates, and they act as core deposits.

For inclusion: they pay implicit interest (fees don't fully cover checking costs), and there's runoff risk.

When interest rates change, do I change my structure at the bank so the bank has to pay me more interest?

Class default: leave demand deposits out of RSLs. In the State Bank solution, savings accounts and MMDAs are counted as rate sensitive within 6 months.

Unequal rate changes

If asset and liability rates move by different amounts, the spread changes too.

ΔNII = (RSA × ΔRRSA) − (RSL × ΔRRSL)

Example 8-2: RSA = RSL = $155M; asset rates +1.2%, liability rates +1.0%. ΔNII = 1.86 − 1.55 = +$310,000, even with zero gap.

Explain results in two pieces, like the State Bank solution. Gap effect: the sign of CGAP times the direction of rates. Spread effect: did asset rates move more or less than liability rates? They can cancel (State Bank part c) or stack (part d).

Weaknesses of the repricing model

  • Ignores market value effects of rate changes.
  • Over-aggregation: buckets are too broad, so mismatches within a bucket are hidden.
  • Ignores runoffs: banks constantly originate and retire loans, and runoff of a "rate-insensitive" item is rate sensitive.
  • Leaves out off-balance-sheet items and hedges like futures.

Macaulay duration

Weighted-average time to maturity, using each cash flow's share of present value as its weight. Measured in years. A more complete measure of rate sensitivity than maturity.

D = Σ(PVₜ × t) / Σ PVₜ

Slide example: $100 loan at 15%, half due at 6 months. CFs 57.50 and 53.75, PVs 53.49 and 46.51, weights 0.5349 and 0.4651. D = 0.5349(½) + 0.4651(1) = 0.7326 years.

Check that your PVs add up to the price. A wrong cell reference in Bonds.xlsx Sheet2 (the top block) only adds to $369.83 instead of $1,000 for that reason.

Features of duration

  • Maturity: duration rises with maturity, at a decreasing rate. Zero-coupon: D = maturity. Every coupon bond: D < maturity.
  • Yield: duration falls as yield rises.
  • Coupon: duration falls as coupon rises.
Going from a maturity of 1 to 2 has more impact than a 30-year bond going to 31.
When my yield increases, I'm discounting all my cash flows more, and the later ones get discounted the most. They count for less in the weighted average, so duration shortens.
Higher coupon: I'm pushing more money earlier in the life of the bond.

Price sensitivity

ΔP/P = −D × [ΔR / (1 + R)] = −MD × ΔR
MD = D / (1 + R)
ΔP = −MD × ΔR × P
Dollar duration = MD × P, so ΔP = −Dollar duration × ΔR
Semiannual coupon bonds: apply the same formula but divide the rate by 2. MD = D / (1 + R/2).

T-bond example (2 years, 8% semiannual coupon, 12% yield): price $930.70, D = 1.8829, MD = 1.776. A 1-point rise: ΔP ≈ −$16.53.

Immunization

Matching maturity to a payout doesn't remove rate risk. Matching duration to the investment horizon does, for small rate shocks.

The goal of immunizing is trying to ensure the value of equity doesn't change.
It drifts. In the problem set, a 3-year 8% bond at 10% has D = 2.777, matching a 2.777-year horizon. One year later the horizon is 1.777, but the bond's duration is 1.925. Duration falls less than one year per year, so you have to rebalance (with 15.92% in 1-year zeros).
Rebalance: Horizon = w × D_zero + (1 − w) × D_bond → w = (H − D_bond) / (D_zero − D_bond)

Duration gap (balance sheet)

E = A − L, so ΔE = ΔA − ΔL. Using duration on each side:

ΔE = −[DA − k·DL] × A × [ΔR / (1 + R)], k = L/A

Three effects: (1) leverage-adjusted duration gap, (2) size of the FI, (3) size of the rate shock.

Example 9-9: DA = 5, DL = 3, A = 100, L = 90, rates 10% → 11%: ΔE = −[5 − 2.7] × 100 × (.01/1.1) = −$2.09.

Unequal rates (assets +1.5 points, liabilities +1 point): ΔA = −6.82, ΔL = −2.45, ΔE = −$4.37.

To set ΔE = 0: DA = k·DL. To protect the E/A ratio instead: DA = DL.
If a problem gives the relative change in rates (State Bank part g), that number already is ΔR/(1+R). Don't divide by (1 + R) again.

Convexity

The price-yield curve isn't a straight line. Duration is the tangent line, so it overstates price drops when rates rise and understates price gains when rates fall. The bigger the rate move, the bigger the error.

Convexity works in the bondholder's favor: when rates fall, prices rise more than duration predicts, and when rates rise, they fall less.

From the In-Class 10-21 tab of Bonds.xlsx: 10-year, 8% annual coupon, $1,000 par, priced at 6% = $1,147.20. Difficulties in applying duration: matching is costly, immunization is dynamic (it drifts), and large rate changes bring in convexity.

FRONT: Formulas

Liquidity
  • NDD = withdrawals − additions
  • FG = Loans − Core deposits = −Liquid + Borrowed
  • Fin. requirement = FG + Liquid assets
  • I = Σ wᵢ(Pᵢ/Pᵢ*) = total sale value ÷ total fair value
  • LCR = HQLA / 30-day net outflows ≥ 100%
  • NSFR = Avail. stable / Req. stable > 100%
Repricing
  • GAP = RSA − RSL; ΔNII = CGAP × ΔR
  • Unequal: ΔNII = RSA·ΔRA − RSL·ΔRL
  • Gap ratio = CGAP / A
Duration
  • D = Σ(PV·t) / ΣPV (semi: R/2, 2n periods, t in years)
  • MD = D/(1+R) (semi: D/(1+R/2))
  • ΔP = −MD·ΔR·P; $Dur = MD·P
  • ΔE = −(DA − kDL)·A·ΔR/(1+R), k = L/A
  • ΔE = ΔA − ΔL (unequal: each side its own ΔR, R)
  • Immunize: DA = kDL; horizon: D = H
  • Rebalance: w = (H − Dbond) / (Dzero − Dbond)

BACK: Logic & Traps

Repricing
  • +GAP: rates ↑ NII ↑. −GAP: rates ↑ NII ↓
  • Unequal rates = gap effect + spread effect (can cancel or stack)
  • RS = matures or resets inside the window; demand deposits = not RS
  • Repricing = book value / NII. Duration = market value / equity
Liquidity
  • PLM = borrow: assets same, NII ↓
  • SLM = cash then sell securities: assets shrink
  • Commitment drawn: assets ↑ under PLM, same under SLM
  • NSFR > 1 = enough funding that's "not going anywhere"
Duration
  • Zero: D = maturity. Coupon bond: D < maturity
  • Coupon ↑ → D ↓. Yield ↑ → D ↓
  • Convexity: D overstates losses, understates gains
  • Immunization drifts: D falls less than 1 yr per yr
  • +DGAP: rates ↑ → equity ↓
